Orientation

The idea in one page

A DXA scan is the machine that tells you whether someone's bones are thinning. This project asks a simple question: using only what a family doctor already has in the room — a scale, a tape measure, a date of birth, a medication list, a few questions about periods and past broken bones, and routine bloodwork — how well can you guess what the DXA scan would have said?

The reason the question is answerable at all is that a large US government survey, NHANES, did both halves for the same people. It interviewed and examined tens of thousands of Americans and gave many of them a hip DXA scan. So for those people we know both the clinic-style inputs and the scan result. That lets us train a model on the inputs, check it against the scan, and then measure how often the guess is right.

Everything the script does falls into four jobs:

  1. Assemble. Pull fourteen different NHANES questionnaire and lab files across six survey rounds, glue them together by person ID, and narrow to one tightly defined group of women.
  2. Label. Convert each woman's hip bone-density measurement into a T-score, the standardised number clinicians use, and from that produce a yes/no label.
  3. Learn. Train eight different models to predict that label from the 15 clinic inputs, keeping the bone-density columns strictly out of the inputs.
  4. Interrogate. Refuse to take the resulting numbers at face value. Compare against a two-variable formula from 2001, re-run the whole model-selection procedure in a way that cannot flatter itself, and translate the result into the only unit a clinician cares about: how many DXA scans you have to order to find one case.

That fourth job is most of the file's length, and it is the part worth showing a specialist. The headline is not "we got AUC 0.81". The headline is what survived being checked.

Background

Why not just scan everyone

Bone thinning is silent. There is no ache, no symptom, nothing a person notices — right up until a hip or a wrist breaks after a minor fall. By then the damage is done, and a hip fracture at 70 is a life-changing event with real mortality attached.

The test that catches it early is a DXA scan: a low-dose X-ray of the hip and spine that measures bone mineral density — how many grams of mineral are packed into each square centimetre of bone. It works well. The problem is not accuracy, it is reach: a DXA scanner is a fixed, expensive machine, appointments have to be ordered and travelled to, and guidelines in many places only trigger a routine scan at 65. Which means the women in the decade before that — exactly the decade when bone loss accelerates fastest, right after menopause — are largely unscanned unless something else flags them.

So the useful question is not "can a model replace DXA" (it cannot, and this project does not claim it). The useful question is triage: given a room full of 55-year-olds and a limited number of scanner slots, can cheap information rank them well enough that the slots go to the right ones?

What "good enough" means here

A triage tool does not have to be right about individuals. It has to order people better than chance and better than whatever is currently used. The script measures exactly that, and then converts it into scans ordered per case found — see Scans per case found.

The central mechanism

Build the answer key, then hide it

This is the one idea that, if the specialist understands nothing else, they should understand. It is also the thing most likely to go wrong in a project like this, and the script is built around preventing it.

Every woman in the dataset has two kinds of information attached to her. The first kind is the DXA result — the bone density numbers DXXNKBMD (femoral neck) and DXXOFBMD (total hip). The second kind is everything a clinic would know without a scanner. The DXA result is used to write the answer key, and then it is deliberately walled off so that no model can see it as an input. If a bone-density column leaked into the inputs, the model would score near perfectly and mean nothing at all — it would be predicting the scan from the scan.

In code, that wall used to be an exclude list: a blocklist naming femoral_neck_bmd, total_hip_bmd, t_femoral_neck, t_total_hip, t_score, bone_status, at_risk and osteoporosis. The rewrite inverted it. FEATURE_PANEL is now an allowlist: fifteen named source variables, and nothing else can reach a model no matter what else the table happens to contain.

Why the direction matters

A blocklist is convenient — a newly engineered variable is picked up automatically with no extra wiring — but it fails in the dangerous direction. Any future column derived from bone density silently becomes an input unless someone remembers to add it to the list, and the failure is invisible: the model simply gets better. An allowlist fails in the safe direction instead. A new column is ignored until someone writes its name down, so the worst outcome is a variable you meant to use sitting idle, not the scan leaking into the thing that is supposed to replace it. The standing leak alarm is still there as a second line; see Seven ways of cheating, guarded.

Steps 1 – 2

Where the data comes from

NHANES — the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey — is a continuous US programme run by the CDC. Each two-year "cycle" recruits a fresh nationally representative sample, interviews them at home, then brings them to a mobile examination centre for measurements, bloodwork and, in some cycles, imaging. Crucially, every person gets one ID number, SEQN, and every data file is keyed on it. That is what makes the whole assembly possible.

The script uses six cycles, listed in the CYCLES constant. NHANES tags file names with a letter per cycle (D = 2005–06, E = 2007–08, and so on), except the combined pandemic-era 2017–2020 release which uses a P_ prefix instead. The code derives every filename from that pattern, so adding a seventh cycle is a one-line change plus a folder of CSVs.

Two files are fetched from the internet, the rest are on disk

The supplement files (DSQTOT_*) and the day-2 diet recalls (DR2TOT_*) are downloaded from wwwn.cdc.gov in NHANES's native SAS transport format and cached locally as CSV. The cache test is "file exists and is bigger than a size floor", which means a truncated half-download gets re-fetched rather than silently used. A failed download is logged and skipped, not fatal. All eleven cache files are committed to the repository, so the pipeline never touches the network again — not even on a fresh clone.

Why day 2 of the diet recall is worth a download

Asking someone what they ate yesterday measures yesterday, not their habitual diet, and the day-to-day scatter is enormous. Feeding a single noisy day into a model systematically drags any real nutrient effect toward zero — the classic regression-dilution problem. Averaging two days does not eliminate it but materially reduces it. On this cohort, 1,040 of the 1,111 women have both days, 63 have one, and 8 have neither.

One quirk that shapes a lot of the code

These CSVs were exported from NHANES with the value labels already applied. So sex is the text "Female", not the number 2; ethnicity is "Non-Hispanic White", not 3; yes/no items are the words "Yes" and "No". That is why the filters accept both forms (isin([2, 2.0, 'Female'])) and why recode_yesno lowercases and matches strings. It also has a quiet consequence: recode_refused, which blanks out the "refused" and "don't know" sentinel codes (7, 9, 77, 99 …), only operates on numeric columns and is therefore a no-op on the label-decoded ones. And because education stays text, the feature builder — which drops all non-numeric columns — silently discards it. Education is collected but never modelled.

Step 5

Making the answer key

A raw bone density of 0.72 g/cm² means nothing on its own. The clinical convention is to express it as a T-score: how many standard deviations a person sits above or below the average for a healthy young adult of the same sex. A T-score of 0 is exactly average for a young adult; −1 is one standard deviation below; −2.5 is two and a half.

The script computes it directly, using fixed NHANES III young-adult reference values held as constants:

Femoral neck
reference mean 0.858 g/cm², SD 0.120
Total hip
reference mean 0.942 g/cm², SD 0.122
T-score used
the lower of the two sites, rounded to three decimals

Taking the minimum of the two sites follows clinical practice — a person is classified by their worst measured site, not their average. The rounding to three decimals is not cosmetic: bone density is reported to three decimals, so without rounding, women sitting exactly on a threshold land on either side of it depending on floating-point noise. An earlier version of this code used pd.cut for the classification and put three women on the wrong side of −2.5 for exactly that reason.

Read this before quoting the headline number

The primary target is not osteoporosis. It is osteopenia-or-worse, which describes 58.1% of this cohort — more than half. A model that predicts it well is answering "does this woman have any measurable bone loss", not "does this woman have osteoporosis". The osteoporosis-only target has just 76 cases against 16 model columns — 4.75 events per predictor, well under the conventional minimum of 10 — so anything estimated on that target alone is fragile. The rewrite made that arithmetic a gate rather than a footnote: power_gate.csv computes events per predictor for every analysis before it runs and labels this target UNDERPOWERED in the output itself. The script still evaluates both targets and reports them side by side rather than picking the flattering one.

Steps 6 – 9

The 15 things the model may look at

Grouped by where a real clinic would get them. This is the honest test of the project's premise: if a variable on this list is not actually obtainable in a primary-care visit, the tool does not work as advertised.

Fifteen variables, sixteen model columns. Fourteen variables are one column each. The fifteenth, current alcohol, enters as the clinical specialist specified it (item 8 of their variable list, Notes/SpecialistUpdate.docx): three categories — zero, fewer than 3, or 3 or more drinks per day — coded as two indicator columns, alcohol_zero and alcohol_3plus, with “<3 per day” (the largest group) as the reference. It replaced the continuous alcohol_drinks_day. An earlier version also carried a categorical menopause_type (natural / surgical / hysterectomy with ovaries kept / unknown) that expanded into three indicator columns. It was removed on the clinical specialist's ruling, so no menopause-type variable is used: postmenopausal status is defined once, by the cohort gate.

Physical activity is not on the headline panel. An earlier version also carried vigorous_activity (PAQ650, yes/no) and sedentary_min_day (PAD680) here, which made it seventeen. Both are missing for the entire 2005–06 cycle — all 173 of its women, because that cycle used the pre-GPAQ activity questionnaire — and median imputation had quietly coded all 173 as "no vigorous activity". That is imputing a structural absence, which the plan rules out. Activity now lives only in its own arms (arms I and I2, below), on the 932 women from 2007 onward who were actually asked.

What an "arm" is

An arm is a planned side-experiment: take the base model (the 15-variable, 16-column panel above), change exactly one thing, refit, and compare the AUC with the base model fitted on the same women and the same folds. The term is borrowed from clinical trials, where each arm is one version of the treatment. Prespecified means the list of arms, and the expected result for each, was written down before anything was run. That is what makes a null reportable: testing variations afterwards and keeping the ones that work is fishing. It holds strictly for arms C–H; arms I, I2 and J were added later, as noted below.

  • C — add calcium + vitamin D intake (930 women)
  • D — add serum 25(OH)D (974)
  • E — add rheumatoid arthritis (988)
  • F — replace the PHQ-9 score with PHQ-9 ≥ 10 (1,111)
  • I — add physical activity: log MET-min/week plus sedentary minutes (932, 2007 onward only). Specified in the rewrite plan and added when the MET variable was implemented, so it postdates the earlier runs rather than predating every run.
  • I2 — add the specialist's secondary activity measures (item 11): log moderate minutes/week, log vigorous minutes/week, plus sitting time (the same 932 women as arm I). Added when implemented from the specialist's list, after arms C–H had been run.
  • J — replace continuous sleep_hrs with the specialist's five sleep bands (item 20, secondary analysis): ≤5 h, 6 h, 7–8 h (reference), 9 h, ≥10 h (1,107). A substitution, never an addition. Also added when implemented from the specialist's list, after arms C–H.
  • G1 / G2 — diabetes recoded with borderline as diabetic, or dropped (1,111)
  • H1 / H2 — outcome taken from the femoral neck only, or the total hip only (1,111). These change the outcome itself, so no difference against the base model is reported.

Before an arm is modelled, power_gate.csv checks it has enough events per predictor (floor 10). Results are in outputs/prespecified_arms.csv.

More variables exist, but only inside a named arm

total_calcium_mg and total_vitd_mcg (930 women), serum_vitd_nmol (974), rheumatoid_arthritis (988) and the activity variables log_met_min_week, log_moderate_min_wk, log_vigorous_min_wk and sedentary_min_day (932) are each measured on a different subset of the cohort. Putting them all in one model silently intersects those subsets and shrinks the analysis to whoever has everything. So the base panel excludes them and each one gets its own prespecified arm, run on its own complete-case sample and compared against the same base panel restricted to the same women. That is the only way the comparison answers "does this variable add anything" rather than "does this subset of women behave differently". None of the five variable-adding arms (C, D, E, I, I2) showed a gain, and neither substitution (F, J) cost anything measurable; every interval contains zero. The numbers are in the arms table.

How the activity variable is built. met_min_week follows the WHO GPAQ convention used in the papers the clinical specialist shared (PMC9858810, PMC12851727): 8 METs × days × minutes for vigorous work (PAQ605/PAQ610/PAD615) and vigorous recreation (PAQ650/PAQ655/PAD660); 4 METs for moderate work (PAQ620/625/PAD630), walking or cycling for transport (PAQ635/640/PAD645) and moderate recreation (PAQ665/670/PAD675); the five domains are summed. A domain answered "No" counts as 0; a blank gate question, or days / minutes outside the valid range (1–7 days, 10–960 minutes per day), leaves the total missing rather than guessed. met_hours_week (÷ 60) is also saved, because that is the unit the papers report. The distribution is heavily skewed (skew ≈ +4; median 720, maximum 43,680 MET-min/week = 728 MET-h/week), so the arm models log_met_min_week = log1p(met_min_week). Coverage is 935 of 1,111; 2005–06 contributes none.

Arm I2 uses the same domains, split by intensity. Following the specialist's secondary measures (item 11), moderate_min_wk sums minutes per week of moderate work, walking or cycling for transport and moderate recreation; vigorous_min_wk sums vigorous work and vigorous recreation. Both enter on the log scale (log1p), alongside sitting time (sedentary_min_day).

Two variables were rescued rather than imputed

PHQ-9 went from 80.4% missing to 0.1%. The old code summed the nine depression items with min_count=5, meaning a woman needed five answered items to get a score — and NHANES routes most respondents past most of those items by skip logic, so four out of five women ended up with no score at all. The rewrite reads the skip pattern instead of fighting it: an unasked item following a negative screen is a zero, not a blank. 1,110 of 1,111 women now have a score, and 112 of them (10.1%) screen positive at the conventional cutoff of 10 — a plausible prevalence, which the 20% sample never was.

sleep_hrs is back in the panel. It was dropped from the old model for data density, on the strength of a coverage figure that was itself an artifact of not coalescing SLD012 (newer cycles) with SLD010H (older ones). Coalesced properly it covers 1,107 of 1,111.

The reproductive variables are the most carefully built part of the pipeline

This is where the domain thinking shows, and it is worth walking a specialist through because it reflects how bone loss actually works rather than what was easiest to code.

  • Years since menopause, not just age. Bone loss accelerates through the menopausal transition and the first years after it, then slows. In a 50–65 cohort where menopause timing varies by a decade or more, chronological age is a weak stand-in for where a woman sits on that curve. The script computes years_since_menopause = current age − age at last period (RHQ060), with implausible ages screened out as sentinel codes. Observed for 1,089 of the 1,111 women; the other 22 are median-imputed inside the training fold. Stated limitation: for a woman whose periods stopped because of a hysterectomy, "age at last period" dates the surgery rather than the end of ovarian function, so her years since menopause can be misstated — and with no menopause-type variable the model cannot tell her apart.
  • No menopause-type variable. Earlier versions separated natural menopause, oophorectomy and hysterectomy with ovaries kept, first as overlapping binaries and then as a single categorical. On the clinical specialist's ruling all of it was dropped: why periods stopped is not modelled in any form, and the hysterectomy / oophorectomy items are no longer used.
  • Still cycling. RHQ031 asks about any period in the last twelve months; the answer is inverted into postmenopausal, which is the cohort gate and the only definition of menopausal status in the pipeline.
One variable was built, then deliberately benched

Built into steps 6 – 11

Seven ways this could have been wrong, and the guard against each

This section is the strongest thing to bring to a specialist meeting. None of these are hypothetical — every one was found in this codebase and fixed, which is a very different and much more credible claim than "we were careful". The first is the one that changed the answer.

1 · The same women counted twice

This is the defect that overturned the project's headline result, and no guard was watching for it. NHANES built its pre-pandemic file by pooling the complete 2017–2018 cycle with the partial 2019–2020 sample and re-numbering every SEQN. So DEMO_J runs 93703–102956 and P_DEMO runs 109263–124822 — disjoint ranges for overlapping people. Loading both cycles, as the old CYCLES list did, put 176 of these women in the cohort twice under two different IDs that no deduplication on SEQN could ever catch.

Cross-validation splits rows, not people. A woman present twice lands in the training fold and the test fold simultaneously, and the model is scored on a row it has already memorised. That is exactly the mechanism target leakage uses, arriving through the front door. The guard is now threefold: 2017–2018 is out of CYCLES; a fingerprint audit hashes each woman's continuous measurements (both BMD values, weight, height, age) and asserts that no two rows share one — this run: 1,306 rows with a complete fingerprint, 0 collisions; and the 2017–2018 file is still read, but only as a lookup table to crosswalk the missing vitamin D values, never as a source of cohort rows.

The cost is quantified rather than asserted. auc_decomposition.csv reruns the whole pipeline at four stages, and the duplicate fix alone accounts for −0.073 AUC — the overwhelming majority of the total −0.081 drop from the published figure. See the honesty section.

2 · Target leakage

Under the old blocklist, a new column derived from bone density would silently become an input. Two guards now sit on this. The structural one is BMD_DENYLIST: ten named columns — both raw BMD values, both site T-scores, the combined T-score, both labels, and the descriptive category — asserted absent from every feature matrix, under every arm, before any model is fitted. The statistical one is a coefficient alarm: after fitting, the script checks the largest standardised coefficient against a threshold of 2.0. The largest legitimate one is about 1.18 (weight_kg); anything carrying the scan would dominate at several times that. This run: largest coefficient 1.179, on weight_kg. No leak signature.

3 · Clinically backwards effects

A dictionary named EXPECTED_SIGNS records the direction each well-established risk factor should point (weight protective, age harmful, prior fracture harmful, and so on). After fitting, any coefficient pointing the wrong way is printed as a warning. This check is what surfaced the age / age-at-menopause collinearity in the first place. This run flags one: bmi, at +0.149 where a protective sign was expected. The script does not merely flag it, it diagnoses it: it reports the flagged variable's strongest collinear partner and that partner's coefficient, and prints "strongest partner weight_kg (|r| 0.94, coef −1.179): partner absorbed the effect, so this is a residual with no clinical reading." At a correlation of 0.94, weight has taken the entire protective effect and BMI is fitting what is left over. The block ablation below confirms this directly — the two together are worth +0.151 AUC, either one alone is worth almost nothing. This is also why the old run's flag on total_vitd_mcg no longer appears: vitamin D intake is no longer in the base panel at all.

Note what is deliberately not in EXPECTED_SIGNS: diabetes. Type 2 diabetes raises measured bone density while also raising fracture risk — the diabetic bone paradox — so the protective coefficient here (−0.046) is the expected result and flagging it would be wrong.

4 · Fabricated totals

Calcium intake is food plus pills. The obvious implementation, .add(fill_value=0), would treat a missing supplement figure as a real zero and produce a confident-looking total from half the information. The script uses plain addition so a missing component yields a missing total, and separately distinguishes "this cycle had no supplement file" (genuinely unobserved, left NaN) from "this woman was surveyed and reported no supplements" (a true zero — NHANES codes non-users as blank, not 0). 173 women unobserved; 181 totals left missing.

The rewrite added a second layer here: per-variable plausibility ranges. A global refused/don't-know list cannot catch a decode error, because NHANES's sentinel codes mean different things in different files. So each variable declares its own valid interval — DR2TCALC 0–8,000 mg, DR2TVD 0–400 µg — and anything outside it is set to missing with a count printed. This run: 3 of 38,222 calcium values rejected, 0 of 29,958 vitamin D values. Near-zero, which is the useful result: the guard confirms the decode was already right rather than papering over a problem.

5 · A variable running under a false name

MCQ160A was previously called rheumatoid_arthritis in this code. It is not rheumatoid-specific — it asks whether a doctor ever mentioned arthritis of any type, and it is positive in 47.7% of this cohort. Rheumatoid arthritis is a genuine FRAX risk factor at a few percent prevalence. Under the wrong name, a common, weakly-relevant variable was masquerading as a rare, strongly-relevant one.

The rewrite went further than renaming it. MCQ195 asks which type of arthritis, so real rheumatoid arthritis is recoverable: 71 of 988 women (7.2%), with 123 excluded because they reported arthritis but no type. That is now a prespecified arm of its own, and any_arthritis — which pools rheumatoid with osteoarthritis, two conditions with opposite bone implications — is off the panel entirely. The arm came back null: Δ +0.000.

6 · Importance measures that lie

Tree-model importances — Gini importance and split counts — were removed from the reporting. They are unsigned and biased toward continuous variables over binary ones, and here they inverted the real ranking: total_calcium_mg placed 7th despite a completely flat outcome gradient across its quartiles (57.8 / 55.0 / 57.6 / 58.5%), while oophorectomy (a flag since dropped from the panel) placed last despite an odds ratio of 1.28. They were replaced with permutation importance — literally: shuffle one column, refit nothing, and measure how much out-of-fold AUC is lost. That is model-agnostic, in the same units for every model, and not biased by variable type.

The rewrite added two more importance methods, because permutation importance has its own blind spot: when two variables carry the same information, shuffling either one costs nothing, since the model simply reads the other. So the script now also runs single-variable ablation (drop a variable, refit everything, remeasure) and block ablation (drop a whole clinical group at once). Where the three disagree is exactly where collinearity lives, and that disagreement is the finding rather than a nuisance — see which variables actually matter.

A file that contradicted this has been removed

feature_weights_matrix.csv, a leftover from a different script, held exactly the tree importance columns that were dropped for being misleading — and looked more authoritative than the correct table because it covered five models. It is gone; see What was removed.

7 · An in-sample score that flatters a forest

A random forest with unrestricted leaves memorises its training rows — its in-sample AUC is close to 1.000 no matter what, and on deliberately shuffled labels this configuration still reaches 0.997. So the train-versus-test gap is meaningless for it. The script enables out-of-bag scoring instead: each row is scored using only the ~37% of trees whose bootstrap sample happened to exclude it. That is the honest in-sample figure for a bagged ensemble, and the leaderboard prints it in its own column with an explicit instruction not to rank models by the train/test gap.

That guard needed a guard of its own. On the osteoporosis target, out-of-bag scoring silently breaks: class_weight='balanced' oversamples the 76 cases so heavily that most of them are in nearly every tree's bootstrap sample and are therefore almost never out-of-bag. Rows with no out-of-bag coverage come back from scikit-learn as all-zero score rows rather than as missing, so they score as confident negatives and the OOB AUC inverts — the unfixed code printed 0.108 in a table labelled PRESENT. The rewrite checks coverage before reporting and withholds the number instead, printing "OOB withheld: only 28% of class 1 rows are ever out-of-bag (class_weight oversamples them)". A refused number is a result; an inverted one is a lie.

And one thing that was tested rather than assumed

The forest's min_samples_leaf was tuned by nested cross-validation, not chosen by eye. It matters because the intuitive move — raising it hard to shrink the alarming train/test gap — does not buy test AUC: leaf = 50 is the lowest point on the grid. The gap is not a defect. Inner CV did not settle on one value (the five outer folds picked 20, 1, 20, 2 and 20), and the sweep rises slightly to leaf = 5 and falls after it: 0.748, 0.750, 0.752, 0.750, 0.749, 0.745 at leaves 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50. The spread is 0.007 AUC against a fold-to-fold SD of about 0.021 — which is the more useful reading of this table than the ranking: on the corrected cohort, this hyperparameter does not matter. That is worth knowing before spending effort tuning it.

Step 10

The models, in plain terms

Eight models across two families. Every one of them is wrapped in the same three-stage pipeline so that the comparison is fair, and — importantly — so that no preprocessing step ever sees the data it will later be tested on:

  1. Impute — fill each missing value with that column's median.
  2. Scale — recentre every column to mean 0, spread 1, so that a weight in kilograms and a yes/no answer are on comparable footing.
  3. Fit — the model itself.

Because the median and the scaling factors are computed inside each fold, they are learned from the training rows only. Computing them once on the whole dataset up front — a very common mistake — would leak information from the test rows into training.

The three regressors — trained on the continuous T-score, never on any label

This arm exists because dichotomising a continuous measurement throws information away. Under the binary label, a woman at T = −2.4 and a woman at T = +0.5 are both simply "negative", which is obviously false. These three models predict the T-score itself, and one fit then serves both clinical cutoffs — you threshold the predicted T-score twice. They are RidgeCV, a random-forest regressor, and a LightGBM regressor.

Why both boosters are deliberately crippled

max_depth=2, num_leaves=4, min_child_samples=50, reg_lambda=50. With 1,111 rows and 16 columns, an unconstrained booster memorises the dataset trivially and reports a fantasy. The heavy regularisation is a decision about the sample size, not a default. Class imbalance is handled per model — class_weight='balanced' for four of them, scale_pos_weight for XGBoost.

How every score is produced: 5-fold cross-validation

Split the 1,111 women into five groups, keeping the proportion of cases equal in each (StratifiedKFold). Train on four, test on the fifth. Rotate five times so every woman is tested exactly once, on a model that never saw her. Average the five results. The random seed is fixed at 42 throughout, so the whole thing reproduces exactly.

The number that takes 0.752's meaning away

By itself, 0.752 is unreadable. The comparison that makes it interpretable is that OST — weight minus age, times 0.2 — scores 0.739 on the same folds, and weight on its own scores 0.737. So fifteen variables and eight models buy about +0.013 AUC over a formula you can do in your head — and once model selection is moved inside the folds, that margin does not survive its own confidence interval. Always quote the two together; quoting the first alone is the single easiest way to misrepresent this project.

Why the three methods disagreed about BMI, and which one was right

bmi is the cleanest illustration in the dataset of why one importance measure is never enough:

  • Odds ratio: 1.12 (0.80 – 1.57), non-significant — and pointing the wrong way, which trips the sign alarm.
  • Permutation importance: +0.0239 in the forest — second highest of all sixteen columns.
  • Single-variable ablation: +0.0002. Ranked 14th of 15. Indistinguishable from deleting nothing.
  • Block ablation, paired with weight: +0.151. The largest effect measured anywhere in this project.

All four are correct, and together they say one thing: BMI and weight are pure substitutes here, correlated at |r| = 0.94 on this cohort. The fitted forest leans on BMI (hence the permutation number), but nothing is lost by taking it away because weight carries the same information (hence the ablation zero), and the odds ratio is unstable for exactly that reason — weight has absorbed the protective effect and BMI is left fitting a residual, which is why its sign inverts. The pair is what matters, which only the block test can see.

What to say when asked "which variables are important?"

Quote the block ablation. It is the only one of the three that answers the question a clinician is actually asking — "what do I need to collect?" — rather than "what did this particular fitted model happen to lean on?". The answer is: a scale, and the patient's age. The other thirteen variables are, on this cohort and at this sample size, not measurably worth collecting.

The clinical picture is otherwise entirely conventional — lighter, older women with a fracture history, lower income and no oestrogen exposure are at higher risk. Nothing here contradicts the osteoporosis literature, which is itself a form of validation: a pipeline this complicated rediscovering the textbook is evidence it is wired up correctly. What it does contradict is the idea that the textbook needed a machine-learning pipeline to find.

Step 12

The honesty checks

An AUC of 0.752 sounds respectable. Three questions decide whether it means anything, and roughly half the script's length exists to answer them. On the corrected cohort, all three answers come back against the project's original claim.

Question one: is it better than a two-variable formula from 2001?

The OST — Osteoporosis Self-assessment Tool — is 0.2 × (weight in kg − age in years). That is the entire model. It was derived by Koh et al. in 2001 from postmenopausal women across eight Asian countries (hence the original name OSTA, “for Asians”), where a variable-selection procedure over the candidate risk factors retained only age and body weight. It has since been externally validated in some nineteen studies, with sensitivity 84–97% and specificity 34–70%, and in non-Asian populations using the same algorithm with locally re-optimised cutoffs. It needs a bathroom scale and a birthdate, and it is the bar any machine-learning model has to clear. The script computes it, plus "weight alone" as an even cruder comparator, and reports every model's AUC relative to OST with a paired bootstrap confidence interval on the difference.

Reporting a bare AUC without that comparison would be uninterpretable. A model that scores 0.752 while OST scores 0.739 has added a great deal of complexity for very little — which, as it turns out, is precisely the situation.

Question two: does picking the winner inflate the winner's score?

Yes, always, and this is the subtlest methodological point in the project. If you fit eight models and report the best one's cross-validated score, that number is biased upward — you chose it because it scored well on those particular rows, and some of that score is luck. The reported best is an upper bound, not an estimate.

The fix is nested cross-validation: move the act of choosing inside the training data, so that no test row is ever involved in selecting the model that will be scored on it.

Both the at_risk and the osteoporosis targets go through this whole apparatus, and the script ends with an explicit verdict for each — "beats OST" only if the nested 95% confidence interval on the difference excludes zero. Its own closing words: "the nested row is the defensible number."

It is guard 1 in full: NHANES re-numbered every SEQN when it built the pre-pandemic file, so loading both the 2017–2018 cycle and the pooled 2017–2020 release put 176 women in the cohort twice under IDs no deduplication could match. Cross-validation splits rows, not people, so those women trained and tested the same model simultaneously.

They agreed because the random forest won in all five folds, and it won in all five folds because duplicated rows reward memorisation. With the duplicates gone, the forest ties with logistic regression and the folds disagree about which model to use.

Why this is still a good result to present, and a better one than before

A project that reported "AUC 0.810, beats OST" would be reporting a number produced by a data defect. This one built the machinery to catch that — a fingerprint audit, a decomposition harness, a power gate, two comparators instead of one — ran it, and printed the uncomfortable verdict itself. Bringing a specialist a null result with the mechanism of the earlier false positive fully traced and quantified is a stronger piece of work than the impressive number was. It also points straight at what the next round needs, which is not a better model: it is either more cases, or a variable that is not another measurement of body size.

A neat side result on regularisation

The leaf-size sweep still points the counterintuitive way, but it no longer points very hard. Per-fold AUC across the grid: 0.748, 0.750, 0.752, 0.750, 0.749, 0.745 at leaves 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50. The old run's much steeper slope (0.810 down to 0.762) was another duplicate artifact: deep trees look dramatically better when memorisation pays.

A technical detail that keeps the nested number honest

Different outer folds can select different winners, and a classifier's output (a probability between 0 and 1) is not on the same scale as a regressor's output (a negated T-score, roughly −1 to 4). Pooling those raw values into a single AUC would corrupt the ordering and deflate the result. So rank_within_folds converts every score to a within-fold percentile before pooling. AUC only uses order, so this changes no within-fold comparison — and it is applied identically to both the model and the OST baseline, keeping the contrast paired.

Steps 12 – 13

Scans per case found

AUC is not a clinical unit. No one orders 0.75 of a scan. The last part of the script translates the model into the decision a clinician actually makes, in two complementary ways.

referral_table — the screening cost table

For each cutoff it reports: how many DXA scans get ordered, how many cases that finds, how many are missed, the positive predictive value, and scans ordered per case found. The last row is always "scan all" — 100% sensitivity at maximum cost — so the trade-off is visible against the alternative of not triaging at all. The rewrite changed what score this table ranks on: it is now the nested-CV out-of-fold rank, not the leaderboard winner's score. That matters, because the leaderboard winner is chosen partly by luck on the rows it is then graded on, and a screening table built on it inherits that optimism.

threshold_sweep — the sensitivity-first sweep

A separate, deliberately different forest: shallower (max_depth=6), larger leaves (min_samples_leaf=15), and weighted 3:1 in favour of catching cases. It is wrapped in CalibratedClassifierCV with sigmoid calibration, which matters: an uncalibrated forest's "0.30" is not a real 30% probability, and if you are going to set a clinical threshold at 0.30 then the number behind it had better mean something. The sweep then walks the cutoff down from 0.50 to 0.25 and reports missed cases and scans flagged at each step.

Why lowering the threshold is the whole point

The default 0.5 cutoff treats a missed case and a false alarm as equally bad. In screening they are not remotely equal: a false alarm costs one unnecessary scan, a missed case costs a hip fracture five years later. Both of these functions exist to let a clinician choose that trade-off explicitly rather than inherit a software default.

Do not put these two tables on the same slide without this caveat

They look contradictory and are not. They differ in two ways at once:

  • Different models. The referral table ranks on the nested-CV out-of-fold score, which is a pooled within-fold percentile and not any single model's output. The sweep uses the separate shallow, calibrated, 3:1-weighted forest, which scores 0.749 ROC-AUC — built for calibrated probabilities rather than maximum ranking.
  • Different meaning of "cutoff". The referral table's "0.50" means flag the top 50% of scores. In the sweep, "≥ 0.50" means a predicted probability of 0.50. The same printed number denotes two different things, which is why each file carries its score type in a column on every row.

There is no longer an osteoporosis referral table. It was withdrawn deliberately: at 76 events the power gate marks that target underpowered, and a screening-cost table implies a precision the sample cannot support. The previous version of this document presented one and called it "where triage actually pays" — that section is gone.

If they ask one question, it will be "so which variables matter?"

This is worth rehearsing, because the answer is short, defensible, and not what the project set out to find. Three methods were run and they agree (the full tables):

  • Body size is very nearly the whole model. Remove bmi and weight_kg together and AUC falls from 0.755 to 0.603 (Δ +0.151 [+0.115, +0.187]). Remove the entire menopause and hormone block and it costs 0.006. The whole lifestyle block (smoking + the alcohol categories): nothing (−0.001). The whole history and family block: 0.004.   • One variable of fifteen survives multiple-comparison correction: weight_kg, +0.0182 AUC when deleted, p < 0.001 after Holm. age is +0.0034. bmi is +0.0002 — because weight already carries it, at |r| = 0.94.

That is the crux: the panel cannot beat OST because OST is already using the two variables that carry the signal; the other thirteen no longer measurably add anything.

Five predictors still show odds ratios whose intervals exclude 1 — weight, prior estrogen use, poverty-income ratio, age and prior fracture — and those are legitimate associations worth discussing. They just do not add discrimination on top of body size, and "associated with the outcome" and "improves the prediction" are different claims.

Questions to expect, and the honest answers

Your outcome is bone density, not fractures. So what?
Correct, and it is the biggest limitation. NHANES is cross-sectional — everyone is measured once, with no follow-up — so no fracture outcome is available. This predicts a test result, not an event. It is a triage tool for who should get scanned, not a fracture-risk calculator like FRAX, and it should not be compared to one.
58% prevalence? That is nothing like my clinic.
Also correct, and it matters for the numbers. The target is osteopenia-or-worse, which is genuinely common in this age group, but NHANES is a survey sample, not a referral population. Sensitivity and specificity travel across settings; positive predictive value and scans-per-case do not. Any referral table would need recalculating against local prevalence.
Why only white women 50–65?
To keep the statistical power focused. With 1,111 people, modelling sex, ethnicity and a wide age range as well would spread the data too thin. The cost is that nothing here generalises outside that group, and the same pipeline would have to be re-run and re-validated for any other population. The narrow cohort is a starting point, not a claim of generality.
How much of this is self-reported?
Most of the questionnaire variables — prior fractures, steroid use, menopause history, smoking, alcohol. Weight, height and blood 25(OH)D are directly measured. Self-report attenuates real effects, which biases toward under-stating the model's ceiling rather than overstating it.
Has it been tested outside this dataset?
No. Everything here is internal cross-validation on one dataset. The nested-CV result is the closest honest estimate of out-of-sample behaviour, but external validation on a genuine clinic cohort has not been done and is the obvious next step.
What about calcium and vitamin D? Surely those matter for bone density.
They are measured here about as carefully as this dataset allows — supplement intake added to a two-day dietary average, plus a blood 25(OH)D level — and none of the three shows measurable value for predicting low bone density in this cohort. Adding intake to the base model changes cross-validated AUC by −0.002 [−0.006, +0.001], an interval containing zero. Adding serum 25(OH)D (974 women) changes it by −0.001 [−0.008, +0.005], also an interval containing zero. An earlier run showed a small significant loss for serum 25(OH)D when a plain forest was that arm's primary model; on this run it is an ordinary null. Both arms are in prespecified_arms.csv.

This is a null result, not a missing measurement, and the distinction matters: the careful measurement is precisely what licenses the statement. It is also consistent with the reverse causation below. What it does not license is any claim about supplementation changing bone density — that needs a trial, not a cross-sectional survey.
Why doesn’t vitamin D help the model?
Neither intake (arm C) nor the blood level (arm D) adds anything measurable. One likely reason is reverse causation: women who have been told their bones are thinning often start taking supplements, which blurs any protective association in a single snapshot. It is a good illustration of why a cross-sectional design cannot settle questions about treatment effects.
Which single number should I trust?
The nested cross-validated difference against OST: +0.008 [−0.008, +0.025] for osteopenia-or-worse, and +0.007 [−0.016, +0.030] for osteoporosis. Both cross zero. Not the leaderboard's 0.752 and 0.793 — those were chosen partly because they looked good on those particular rows — and certainly not 0.810 or 0.833, which were computed on the duplicated cohort and are withdrawn. The four-row nested_cv_verdict.csv is the whole bottom line and it is the first file to open.
Is a two-variable formula really the benchmark?
It is the right benchmark precisely because it is so cheap: weight minus age, times 0.2. It scores 0.739 here for osteopenia and 0.780 for osteoporosis — both inside its published range, which is a check on the implementation. A model that needs fifteen variables — and, in its arms, blood work and a diet survey — has to justify itself against that, and on this cohort it does not. We also ran a harsher second comparator, weight alone (0.737 / 0.766). The panel is not separable from that either: +0.010 [−0.007, +0.027] for osteopenia and +0.021 [−0.005, +0.048] for osteoporosis. (The previous run had the osteoporosis comparison just clearing zero; it no longer does.) If a specialist prefers FRAX as the comparator, that is a fair challenge — FRAX is not implemented here, and it predicts fracture rather than density, so it is not directly substitutable.
So did the project fail?
No — it produced a negative result and caught its own error, which is a different thing. The claim it started with does not survive; what it can say now is narrower and much more solid: on a clean cohort of 1,111 postmenopausal women, clinic-available questionnaire data adds nothing measurable to a scale and a date of birth for predicting hip bone density. That is worth knowing, it is worth writing down, and it is the kind of finding that does not get published often enough. The defect that produced the earlier number is documented, quantified and fixed in code (guard 1), and every artifact on disk now traces to the corrected cohort.

Two things to ask them for

  1. The cutoff. The referral tables lay out the trade-off, but the acceptable number of missed cases per hundred, and the acceptable number of scans per case found, is a clinical judgement — not one to make from the code.
  2. Missing risk factors. Height loss, current smoking intensity and weight change since 40 are all established and all absent here. (Parental hip fracture is in the panel now, at 4.4% missing; deleting it changes AUC by −0.0003, nothing measurable.) If any of the absent three are routinely in their notes, they are candidates for the next iteration — though the ablation grid sets a low prior: thirteen of fifteen existing variables add nothing measurable, so a sixteenth would have to be carrying information that body size does not.
The obvious objection

Why the benchmark is OST and not FRAX

FRAX runs on much the same primary-care inputs — age, sex, weight, height, prior fracture, steroids, smoking, alcohol — and can be computed without a DXA. So the first question any specialist will ask is why this project measures itself against a two-variable formula from 2001 instead of the tool everybody actually uses.

The answer is that they predict different things, for different decisions, at different points in the care pathway.

FRAX answers “should I treat?”; this model answers “should I scan?” Those are sequential decisions, not competing ones.

Two reasons FRAX could not be the comparator here

  • The outcome does not match. OST was built to predict low BMD. FRAX was built to predict fractures. Benchmarking a BMD-prediction model against FRAX would measure it against a tool designed for a different target — and would flatter or punish it for reasons that have nothing to do with how well it does its own job.
  • NHANES cannot support it anyway. Most FRAX inputs are in the survey (age, weight, height, prior fracture, parental hip fracture, smoking, steroids, rheumatoid arthritis, alcohol), but FRAX predicts a ten-year fracture risk, and checking it needs fracture follow-up, which a cross-sectional survey has none of. The outcome FRAX is built for is not available.

And FRAX is known to be weak at this particular job

This is the part worth bringing to the meeting, because it is not a matter of opinion — using FRAX-without-BMD to decide who needs a DXA has been tested directly, and in this project’s exact age band it performs poorly.

FRAX-without-BMD is not a proxy for a T-score, and the literature is explicit that its performance target should be fracture risk rather than BMD classification.
The strongest framing: complementary, not competing

FRAX performs better with a BMD value than without one. So a tool that improves who gets scanned improves the quality of the input FRAX depends on downstream. That is a coherent clinical story and it requires no claim of superiority over FRAX at all — which is just as well, because this project never tested fracture prediction and could not have.

Say this before anyone else does

More than 75% of people who sustain a fragility fracture do not meet the T ≤ −2.5 threshold. Bone density is an imperfect surrogate for the thing anyone actually cares about, so predicting it well bounds what this model can ever claim. That is an argument for eventually predicting fractures rather than densities — which needs a prospective cohort this project does not have, and is the single best reason to seek a clinical collaborator.
