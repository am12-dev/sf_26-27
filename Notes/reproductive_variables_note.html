Reproductive variables: one count, and one problem

Two things to settle. First, the cohort count if menopause is defined simply as “no menstrual period in the last 12 months”. Second — and more important — the variable years_since_menopause is measuring the wrong event for about a third of the cohort, and the fix needs your judgement on which construct you actually want.

1  The count

Menopausal status comes from RHQ031: “Have you had at least one menstrual period in the past 12 months?” Answering no is the standard twelve-month-amenorrhoea definition.

A design point worth deciding deliberately

Menopause can be an inclusion criterion or a predictor, but not both. Restricting the cohort to postmenopausal women makes the group clinically cleaner and improves several variables — but it also removes the contrast that lets the model measure what menopause contributes. If the study question is which variables identify low bone mass, menopause has to stay a variable. If the question is how do we screen postmenopausal women, it should be a filter. Both are defensible; they answer different questions.

The current pipeline keeps all 1,484 and carries menopause as a binary predictor.

One genuine benefit of filtering, if you choose it

Most of the missing values in years_since_menopause belong to women who had not been through menopause and so had no age at last period to give. Filtering removes them, and missingness falls from 15.8% to 1.9%. menopause_natural improves from 33.6% to 22.6%, and ever_used_estrogen from 2.9% to 0.2%.

2  The problem with years since menopause

It is measuring the wrong event for a third of the cohort

The variable is computed as current age − age at last menstrual period, where the second term is RHQ060: “How old were you when you had your last menstrual period?”

In a cohort capped at 65, the maximum value is 42 years — implying a last period at age 23. That is what prompted this note, and the explanation is mechanical: a hysterectomy ends menstruation whether or not the ovaries keep working. A woman whose uterus was removed at 23 but who kept her ovaries went on producing oestrogen for another twenty-five years. This variable records her as forty-two years post-menopause.

The no-surgery group lands exactly where it should — median 50, and 3% before 40, consistent with the population rate of premature ovarian insufficiency. The variable is trustworthy for these women.
The scale of it

Of the 224 women recorded as reaching menopause before 40, 194 had a hysterectomy and only 29 had neither surgery. So roughly 87% of the apparent premature menopause in this dataset is a surgical artefact, not early ovarian failure.

460 women — 31% of the cohort — had a hysterectomy, and for all of them this variable is dating the wrong event. This is not a handful of outliers.

It compounds two problems already on the table

years_since_menopause is the only variable in the study whose missingness is associated with the outcome — women with a missing value have a 16.4 percentage point lower rate of low bone mass (p < 0.001). Now its observed values are also compromised for a third of the cohort.

And since hysterectomy was removed from the model on clinical advice, there is no longer any variable telling the model which women are affected.

3  The fix, using data already downloaded

Three groups, three different correct answers

Two NHANES variables that record the age at surgery are present in five of the six cycles and are currently unused. With them, each group can be dated correctly:

GroupCorrect age of oestrogen lossSource
Natural menopauseAge at last period — valid as recorded RHQ060, all 6 cycles
Bilateral oophorectomy Age at the operation — this is when oestrogen actually stopped RHQ332, 5 of 6 cycles, currently unused
Hysterectomy, ovaries retained Unknown. Menstruation stopped; oestrogen production did not. There is no valid date —
RHQ291 (age at hysterectomy) is also available and confirms the pattern: it is populated for 96.6% of women reporting a hysterectomy and 0% of those who did not.

Applying this would leave roughly 179 women with an honestly missing value instead of a confidently wrong one, and would give the oophorectomy group a date that reflects the biology rather than the surgery type.

Also unused, and relevant to the same question

RHQ560Q and RHQ560U record how long hormone therapy was taken, in five of the six cycles. At present ever_used_estrogen is a plain ever/never flag — and it is one of only five predictors in the whole study that reaches statistical significance (odds ratio 0.58, protective). Six months of HRT twenty years ago and fifteen years of continuous use are currently the same value.

What we need from you

Four decisions

  1. Cohort or predictor? Keep all 1,484 women and carry menopause as a binary variable, or restrict to the 1,272 confirmed postmenopausal and drop it as a predictor?
  2. Which construct do you want? Is the clinically meaningful quantity years since the last menstrual period, or years since oestrogen production stopped? They are the same number only for women who had no surgery.
  3. The hysterectomy-with-ovaries group. For these 179 women, should the value be set to missing, estimated from the population average age at menopause, or left as recorded with the limitation documented?
  4. Is duration of hormone therapy worth adding? It would sharpen the strongest protective predictor in the study, at the cost of one more variable with incomplete coverage.
Already actioned from your previous review

hysterectomy has been removed as a predictor, as you advised — a hysterectomy with the ovaries retained does not induce menopause, and the data agreed (adjusted odds ratio 0.96, 0.68–1.42). Note the interaction with point 3 above: removing it as a predictor is right, but the pipeline still needs to know who had one in order to date their menopause correctly.
