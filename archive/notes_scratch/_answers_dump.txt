[Heading1] Two terms, so the results make sense when they arrive
[ListBullet] Accuracy (the c-statistic, sometimes written AUC): we report how well a score separates women who do have low bone density from those who do not.
[ListBullet] “With and without”: For every variable we run the whole analysis twice, once using the variable and once leaving it out, and report how much accuracy changes. That shows what each variable contributes, not merely whether it looks statistically significant.
Every score is always tested on women who were not used to build it, so the accuracy figures are not flattered by the data they came from.

Q3. Is 1,272 the group we report?
You asked us to remove anyone with a cycle in the last twelve months. Q4. Do you also want the analysis repeated on all 1,484? And how should years since menopause be defined after hysterectomy?
Two parts. First: we can repeat everything including the 212 excluded women, but it needs care, because adding them makes the group easier to sort quite apart from anything we do. Scoring both groups on age alone, with nothing fitted, accuracy rises from 0.555 to 0.588; the OST formula rises from 0.733 to 0.747. A higher figure on 1,484 would therefore reflect an easier mix of patients, not a better score. If the ovaries were kept, estrogen continued afterwards, so the figure overstates true estrogen-deficient years — the quantity that actually drives bone loss. Q8. Diabetes runs opposite to your prediction — does our proposed approach match what you want?
Your note treats diabetes as a risk factor for lower bone density. In our women it goes the other way: mean T-score −0.807 in diabetics against −1.211 in non-diabetics, and 5.4% osteoporosis against 7.5%. The explanation is body weight — diabetic women here average 5.7 kg/m² more BMI, and once age and BMI are accounted for, diabetes has no independent relationship with T-score at all (p = 0.79). This matches the recognised type-2 picture of normal or high bone density alongside raised fracture risk, which a DXA T-score cannot show; and this survey does not distinguish type 1 from type 2. There is also a hard limit: the diabetic group contains only 7 cases of osteoporosis, far too few to build a separate score on. We propose describing the two groups side by side, testing whether diabetes changes the effect of BMI, and building the score in non-diabetic women (1,109 women, 83 cases), with the diabetic group reported for the lower threshold only and clearly marked as underpowered. Does that match what you want?
