NHANES osteoporosis screening study · methods note

One missing value, traced end to end

A patient has every variable recorded except her calcium intake. She is not dropped from the study — her calcium is filled in with a single number. This note follows exactly what that number is, where it comes from, and what it costs. The calcium chain is the right example to work through, because it is the most layered one in the pipeline and because the answer turns out to be wrong in an avoidable way.

The short answer

She stays in the study. Her missing total_calcium_mg is replaced with 1,030.83 mg/day — the median of every other woman's total calcium intake — and the model then treats that number exactly as if it had been measured. Nothing in the model records that it was invented.

That is the whole mechanism. The rest of this note is about why the specific number 1,030.83 is the wrong one to use for her, and what to do instead.

Why not simply exclude her?

Because excluding anyone with an incomplete record removes the entire cohort. All 1,484 women are missing at least one of the 22 variables, so a complete-case rule retains zero patients. Even relaxing it to 21 variables leaves only 484. Imputation is not a convenience here; it is what makes the study possible at all.

How calcium is assembled, in four stages

Total calcium is not a single NHANES question. It is built from three separate sources, each with its own rule for what counts as unknown.

1

The two dietary recalls are averaged, and one is enough

Calcium from food comes from two separate 24-hour recalls, taken on different days. The pipeline averages them, and the averaging skips missing values — so a woman with only day 1 keeps day 1 rather than losing both. 1,369 women have both days, 90 have one, 25 have neither. Two days are used rather than one because a single day measures that day, not habitual intake.

2

Supplements distinguish “takes none” from “never asked”

NHANES records a non-user as blank, not as zero, so a blank is ambiguous. The pipeline resolves it by cycle: if her survey cycle ran the supplement questionnaire at all, a blank means she reported no calcium supplement and becomes 0. If her cycle never ran it, the blank stays unknown. The 2005–2006 cycle has no supplement file, so all 206 of its women are unknown.

3

The total is a plain sum, and this is deliberate

total_calcium_mg = dietary average + supplement, with no fill-in. If either part is unknown the total is unknown. This is the correct choice in principle — treating an unknown supplement as zero would manufacture a confident-looking total from half the information. 229 of 1,484 totals end up missing.

4

At model time, the median fills the gap

Every model runs through the same pipeline: median imputation, then standardisation, then the estimator. Our patient's blank total becomes 1,030.83 mg/day. The median is computed on the training rows of each cross-validation fold only, never on the held-out rows, so no information leaks from test to train.

The avoidable problem

204 of the 229 women have a measured dietary calcium intake, and it is being thrown away. Their dietary intake has a median of 764 mg/day. Because the total is what enters the model, and the total is blank, the imputer overwrites the whole thing with the cohort median of 1,031 mg/day.

So a real measurement of 764 is replaced by an invented 1,031 — roughly 267 mg/day too high, about a third of a glass of milk, in a consistent direction. A more defensible fill would be her known diet plus the median observed supplement intake (53 mg), giving 817 mg/day.

And the error is systematic, not scattered

206 of the 229 are the entire 2005–2006 cycle. This is not random noise distributed across the study — it is one survey round being handed a fabricated intake value, all in the same direction. The group also differs from the rest: 51.5% have low bone mass against 57.2% among women whose calcium is known.

What the model does and does not know

Ordinarily an imputed value is indistinguishable from a measured one — the model receives a number with no note attached. For calcium there is a partial exception, and it arrived by accident.

A variable called supp_data_available is in the feature set, and it is 0 for exactly the 206 women whose cycle ran no supplement questionnaire. It was added to record a fact about the dataset, not as an imputation flag — but it functions as one, so for this particular cause of missingness the model can tell that the calcium value is synthetic.

Two consequences worth stating plainly

For the other 23 women — those with no usable dietary recall — there is no flag at all, and their imputed calcium is indistinguishable from a measurement.

supp_data_available is otherwise a poor thing to have as a predictor: it describes which survey round a woman was in, not anything about her body. Its odds ratio is 1.00 and its predictive contribution is nil. The recommendation elsewhere in this project is to remove it — which would also remove the accidental flag, so the two decisions have to be taken together.

Worth noting on the current approach's behalf

The pipeline already does two things correctly that are commonly got wrong. The imputer is fitted inside each cross-validation fold, so no information crosses from test to train. And the bootstrap used for the odds ratios refits imputation on every replicate, so the reported confidence intervals do carry imputation uncertainty rather than pretending the filled values were observed. The AUC figures do not carry it.

The question for the specialist

Calcium has no measurable association with bone density in this cohort — odds ratio 1.01 (0.90–1.11), and removing it entirely changes cross-validated accuracy by less than the fold-to-fold noise. So the practical question is whether it is worth fixing carefully or simply dropping.

The argument for fixing it is that a well-measured null is a finding: you can only say “calcium intake showed no association” if the measurement was sound. Right now, for 204 women, it is not.
